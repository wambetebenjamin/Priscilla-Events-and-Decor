Most palette problems are not taste problems. They are structure problems. Here is the method we
walk clients through, in the order we walk through it.

## Start with the venue, not with Pinterest

The venue has already chosen two or three colours for you. The floor is a colour. The walls are a
colour. The curtains, the ceiling, the chairs if they stay, and the view through the windows are
all colours you are working with, not against.

Photograph your venue at the time of day your event will happen. Put that photograph next to your
saved inspiration. If the two look like they belong to different events, that is the first thing to
solve.

## Build three tones, not five colours

A palette that works has three jobs filled:

1. **The base.** The largest area: linen, flooring, draping. Usually cream, ivory or a warm neutral.
2. **The support.** A mid tone that carries the theme: blush, sand, sage, terracotta, navy.
3. **The accent.** The small, confident colour that appears in flowers and in a few details.

If every colour in your palette is doing the same job, nothing stands out. If five colours are
competing for the same area, the room looks busy in person and muddy in photographs.

## Test it in the light you will actually have

A palette that sings at 4pm in a garden can go grey at 8pm in a hall. Warm light, meaning anything
at or below about 3000K, pulls colours toward yellow. Cool white light pushes them toward blue.

This matters most for cream, blush and grey, which are exactly the colours most couples choose.
Ask your decorator what the lighting plan is before you finalise the palette, and ask to see the
linen sample under that light, not in a showroom.

## Use the 60, 30, 10 split

Roughly: sixty percent base, thirty percent support, ten percent accent. It is not a rule of law,
but it is a reliable instinct check.

If flowers are your main spend, they should be the accent, not the base. A room where every surface
is covered in the same colour has nothing to look at.

## Let one element be the exception

Every memorable room we have dressed has one thing that breaks its own palette on purpose. A
midnight navy napkin on a cream table. A brass candlestick in a garden. One deep plum arrangement
in a room of blush.

The exception is what makes the palette read as a decision rather than a default.

## Where people usually go wrong

- **Choosing colours from a screen.** Phone screens over-saturate, and every phone does it
  differently. Ask for physical samples of anything you are spending real money on.
- **Matching the bridesmaids exactly.** Near matches in the same tone family look more considered
  than identical dye lots, and they are far easier to source.
- **Forgetting the food and the flowers.** Salmon, greens and red wine all sit on the table. A
  palette that ignores them will clash with them.
- **Too many materials.** Colour is only half of it. Matte linen with glossy glass and dull brass
  reads richer than five colours in the same plastic finish.

## A shortcut that works

If you are stuck, choose the palette from the venue's hardest fixed element, which is usually the
floor, and then take your accent from the season you are marrying in. January in Nairobi suggests
greens and creams. August suggests deeper, cooler tones. You will be working with the light and the
season rather than arguing with them.
